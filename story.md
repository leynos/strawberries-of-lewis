## The Strawberry Bunkers of Lewis

By 2043, every child on Lewis knew the shape of the atom.

Not the tidy schoolbook version, with electrons swarming politely around a nucleus like midges around a tourist’s face. They knew the atom as architecture: concrete plinths, turbine halls, coolant loops, modular containment, a white steam plume rising into a sky the colour of old pewter. They knew it as the reason their grandparents stopped saying, “There’s no work for you here, mo chridhe,” and started saying, “There’s a shift going in Barvas, but don’t let them put you on nights.”

The Rolls-Royce SMR arrived in three convoys and one argument.

The convoys came up from Arnish before dawn, escorted by police, engineers, three documentary crews, and a councillor who had practised the phrase *sovereign clean energy transition* until it sounded like something she had invented in a fever. The argument happened because nobody had told Calum Macleod of Coll that the road past his auntie’s croft would close for six hours, and Calum had a vet coming to look at a tup with a suspicious limp.

“This is what happens when you let English reactors loose on an island,” he told BBC Alba, with the serene fury of a man who knew he had found his angle.

The reactor itself did not look dramatic. That disappointed people. They had expected fins, a humming blue glow, perhaps a warning siren that sounded like the end of history. Instead, it came in sections: thick cylinders, dull metal skins, pallets of control equipment, sealed containers with printed codes and quiet men who kept touching tablets and nodding at one another.

It took five years to build, certify, test, argue about, delay, re-certify, bless, curse, and finally switch on.

When it did, Lewis changed temperature.

Not literally, although some claimed they could feel it. The island had always lived in negotiation with wind. Wind salted the windows. Wind bullied the ferry. Wind lifted wheelie bins and spread secrets from one end of Stornoway to the other. Wind had made Lewis famous for the wrong kind of energy, the kind that arrived sideways and left you poorer in slate.

But now the island had baseload.

That was the word everyone learned. Baseload. A word with square shoulders. A word that could stand in the doorway and stop uncertainty getting in.

At first, the power went where everyone expected: heat pumps, district heating, electrolysers, fish processing, data centres, ferries with batteries heavy enough to shame a cathedral bell. Then the Agricultural Transition Board, which had started as a joke in a council meeting and somehow acquired a budget, proposed the greenhouses.

Nobody on Lewis trusted the word *greenhouse*. It sounded too southern, too full of weak tea and cucumber sandwiches. A greenhouse belonged in a garden in Surrey with a retired magistrate and a citrus tree called Nigel.

These did not.

The first one rose outside Tong like a military installation designed by a botanist with trauma. It had low arched roofs under turf and stone, blast-deflecting buttresses, triple-layer polycarbonate set behind retractable storm shields, and doors thick enough to survive either a hurricane or a theological dispute. Local children called it Strawberry Bunker One. The name stuck with such violence that the official project title, Hebridean Protected Horticultural Unit Alpha, died within a week.

Inside, summer had been captured and put on a wage.

Rows of strawberries grew under calibrated light, their leaves dark and glossy, their flowers white and neat. Heat from the SMR moved through insulated pipes beneath the grow beds. Sensors tasted the air. Robots the size of spaniels rolled between lanes, examining fruit, whispering to each other in compression bursts. Bees lived in amber-lit cabinets, released on schedule, unionized in spirit if not in law.

The strawberries themselves were ridiculous.

They grew fat and bright while Atlantic rain threw gravel at the roofs. They smelled of June in a place where June often smelled of wet fleece. The first official punnet went to the care home on Matheson Road, where Mairi NicLeòid, aged ninety-six, ate one berry, shut her eyes, and said, “Well. That’s no natural.”

Then she ate seven more.

The mainland noticed.

Chefs in Glasgow began serving Lewis strawberries with crowdie, sea salt, and sentences too long for menus. Edinburgh restaurants invented tasting courses in which one berry arrived under a glass dome full of peat smoke, causing three food critics to write about memory and one to write, simply, “I have been emotionally mugged by dessert.” London buyers tried to secure exclusive supply. They failed. The island had spent centuries watching other people take things away from it. This time, Lewis set the terms.

The problem was transport.

The ferry remained the ferry. It still carried people, cattle, vans, impatience, rumours, replacement parts, and the occasional coffin. It could not carry fragile berries at the pace that the market demanded. By 2047, the strawberries had become too valuable to sit in Ullapool because the Minch had decided to practice violence.

That was when the UAVs arrived.

They called them gannets, because they had long folding wings, bright white fuselages, black wingtips, and the patient predatory stare of seabirds that knew exactly where lunch had made its final mistake. Each could carry six insulated cargo pods under the belly and skim to the mainland on electric rotors and hydrogen-assisted cruise. On clear days, they ran to Ullapool, Inverness, Oban, Dundee, even Prestwick. On bad days, which were most days with ambitions, they flew anyway.

The pilots worked from a container farm on the outskirts of Stornoway.

That phrase confused visitors. They pictured lettuce. There was no lettuce. The container farm consisted of forty-eight stacked shipping containers painted in regulation municipal optimism: sea blue, lichen green, heather purple, nuclear white. Some housed servers. Some housed simulators. Some housed break rooms where twenty-year-olds ate crisps, drank energy drinks, and argued over whether a drone that landed itself could truly count as having “landed well”.

The youngest pilots had grown up in the long adolescence of permanent war streams, spy dramas, leaked cockpit footage, and games where every mission began with a voice saying, “Langley has eyes on target.” At fourteen, they had imagined themselves flying black triangles over deserts for the CIA, deciding the fate of men with beards, trucks, and suspiciously rectangular parcels.

Instead, they flew strawberries to chefs named Piers.

The glamour had rearranged itself.

Their leader, if such a word could apply to someone who wore pink headphones and kept a rubber duck on her console, was Eilidh “Switch” Morrison. She had been the best drone racer in the Hebrides by twelve, the best liar about homework by thirteen, and by seventeen had won a Ministry of Transport scholarship after landing a training UAV on the back of a moving ferry during a crosswind simulation that made the examiner swear in two languages.

Switch had wanted the CIA very badly for about eighteen months.

This came from a phase involving American podcasts, mirrored sunglasses, and a conspiracy board on her bedroom wall that connected Inverness Airport, the Bilderberg Group, and her maths teacher. Her mother had tolerated it until Switch tried to order tactical gloves online. Then she had said, “You are not joining an intelligence agency that does not know where Garrabost is.”

So Switch joined Hebridean Air Agrilogistics instead.

The job paid better than espionage, had more predictable toilets, and involved fewer moral injuries. Still, she occasionally narrated missions in an American accent when bored.

“Gannet Four, you are cleared hot on target dessert menu. Repeat, target dessert menu.”

“Switch,” said Iain from the next pod, “that’s a delivery to a wedding in Pitlochry.”

“High-value civilian happiness package. CIA would kill for this routing.”

“CIA would crash into Ben Wyvis because they’d call it Ben Why-vis.”

“Fair.”

Their control room glowed blue and green in the dark. Outside, the moor rolled away in wet browns and electric golds, fence posts leaning under the old gossip of wind. Inside, the pilots sat in ergonomic chairs beneath wall screens that showed cloud layers, bird movements, ferry traffic, turbine wakes, military exclusion zones, and the little bright icons of their gannets crossing the Minch.

The SMR fed them power. The bunkers fed them cargo. The island fed them weather.

Weather, on Lewis, did not behave like background. It had opinions. It came to meetings. It disputed invoices.

On the morning of the Great Strawberry Run, the weather wore three fronts and a smirk.

A cargo contract had come in from the mainland consortium: twelve tonnes of berries for synchronized delivery to restaurants, hospitals, schools, and the Scottish Parliament. The Parliament order had caused arguments. Half the island wanted to send the best berries as proof of excellence. The other half wanted to send the weird tiny ones from the experimental row where the AI had briefly misunderstood “compact fruit”.

The best berries won, narrowly.

At 05:40, Strawberry Bunkers One through Nine began loading. Robotic pickers selected fruit by sugar, firmness, colour, and aromatic profile. Human inspectors vetoed anything that looked too smug. The pods chilled and sealed. Vans with narrow tyres rolled from the bunkers to the launch strip, headlights crawling through rain.

Switch arrived late, wearing yesterday’s eyeliner and a jacket she had stolen from an ex who still followed her flight logs. She carried a coffee so strong it seemed capable of voting.

“What’s the sky doing?” she asked.

Iain looked at the model. “Trying to invent a new direction.”

On the central screen, the Minch foamed white under a bruised cloud shelf. Wind came from the west at thirty-eight knots, gusting fifty-two. The low-level corridor to Ullapool flickered amber. The northern diversion over Loch Broom pulsed red. A military exercise had closed a wedge of airspace east of Skye, because somewhere in Whitehall a colonel had decided Tuesday needed more expensive noises.

Switch considered the map.

“Launch six to Inverness. Hold two. Route Four and Seven south of the worst shear. Send Gannet Nine high and lazy. She likes drama.”

“She’s an aircraft,” said Iain.

“She has a personality and you know it.”

At 06:10, the first gannet lifted.

It rose almost vertically, rotors chopping rain into silver dust, then folded into forward flight and vanished toward the sea. The next followed. Then the next. Across the island, people looked up from kitchens, buses, sheep pens, school gates, and the harbour, hearing the soft ascending whine that had become the sound of Lewis exporting summer.

Inside the control farm, the kids flew.

Not children now, not quite. They had mortgages, tattoos, bad backs, pension contributions, and group chats with names no one would want read aloud in court. But to the older islanders they remained “the drone kids”, the ones who had learned sticks before steering wheels, the ones who could read wind shear from a tremor in telemetry.

Switch took manual assist on Gannet Seven when she hit turbulence north of the Summer Isles. The UAV bucked. Its cargo pods registered micro-shocks. A warning bloomed.

“Come on, darling,” Switch murmured. “No jam today.”

She dropped altitude, rode the smoother air above the water, then banked hard to avoid a flock of geese whose collective navigation strategy appeared to involve spite. The gannet’s wing flexed. For half a second the feed blurred with spray.

Iain stopped eating.

“Switch?”

“I’ve got her.”

Her fingers moved lightly. No panic. No cinema. Just work. She let the aircraft fall ten metres, caught lift along the wave front, then trimmed into a narrow corridor between two ugly parcels of wind. Gannet Seven steadied.

The cargo temperature held. The strawberries slept in their pods like aristocrats in red silk pyjamas.

At 06:58, Gannet Three landed at Raigmore Hospital with punnets for patients who had not tasted fresh soft fruit in weeks. At 07:12, Gannet Six touched down outside a school near Dingwall, where children screamed with the primal joy of seeing a robot bird bring snack time. At 07:40, Gannet Nine reached Edinburgh after a leisurely high-altitude route and descended over a city that had once thought itself the centre of Scottish cleverness.

By 08:05, the Parliament kitchen had received its berries.

By 08:07, someone had posted a picture captioned: *Nuclear strawberries from Lewis. The future tastes smug.*

By 08:16, three newspapers had called.

By 08:22, Calum Macleod of Coll phoned a radio programme to say the whole thing would have been impossible if they had not shut the road past his auntie’s croft in 2038, and that nobody had ever thanked the tup.

The story might have ended there, in commerce and weather and mildly radioactive jokes, except for what happened at 09:03.

Gannet Four disappeared.

Not exploded. Not crashed. Not failed in any normal, paperwork-friendly way. Its icon blinked twice over the Minch, then vanished from the civilian tracking display.

The control room went still.

“Telemetry?” Switch asked.

“Lost.”

“Transponder?”

“Gone.”

“Last ping?”

“Fifteen kilometres west-northwest of Loch Ewe.”

The old fantasies crept back into the room. The drone kids looked at one another with the private electricity of people who had once imagined secret missions. The CIA version of the world, the one with hidden hands and meaningful static, briefly leaned over their shoulders.

Then Kirsty, who had a degree in aerospace systems and no tolerance for narrative excess, said, “Probably antenna icing.”

Switch nodded. “Probably.”

But her eyes had narrowed.

They pulled satellite weather. They queried maritime radar. They checked military NOTAMs. They pinged the maintenance mesh. Nothing. Gannet Four had carried two hundred kilos of top-grade strawberries destined for a hotel in Inverness, a Michelin-starred restaurant in Leith, and one primary school in Aviemore whose headteacher had written the most emotionally manipulative procurement email in Hebridean history.

At 09:19, an encrypted signal arrived on a maintenance channel that nobody had used since commissioning.

Text only.

> CARGO SECURE. PILOT LOCAL. REQUEST LANDING PERMISSION.

Iain whispered, “That’s not our protocol.”

Switch leaned closer. “No. It’s older.”

The message included coordinates. Not Loch Ewe. Not the mainland. Not even sea.

It pointed back to Lewis.

To an abandoned MOD listening station north of Ness, a concrete wart on the moor left over from an age when the British state had believed everything worth knowing could be heard through aerials.

Switch’s smile arrived slowly.

“No,” said Kirsty.

“Yes,” said Switch.

“No.”

“Gannet Two is light and fuelled.”

“You are not launching a recovery flight because someone is pretending to be a spy.”

Switch put on her pink headphones.

“They stole my strawberries.”

Gannet Two lifted into rain.

Its camera found the old station at 09:41. The site had been empty for years, except for sheep, teenagers, and the occasional photographer trying to make decay look profound. Now one of the concrete bays had opened. Gannet Four sat inside, intact, wings folded. Beside it stood three figures in waterproofs.

One held up a cardboard sign.

SORRY. NEEDED TO TEST SYSTEM.

Switch zoomed in.

The figure turned the sign around.

ALSO WE WERE BORED.

Kirsty made a sound only engineers make when confronted by creativity with legal consequences.

The culprits turned out to be retired signals technicians. Two had worked on submarine communications. One had helped install the island’s first SMR cyber monitoring array. All three belonged to a model aircraft club with a membership dispute so venomous that the Free Church had declined to host mediation.

They had not stolen the gannet, they insisted. They had “demonstrated an exploitable legacy channel”. They had landed it safely. They had kept the strawberries cold. They had intended to inform the authorities after breakfast.

“Why?” Switch asked them later, standing in the concrete bay while Gannet Four blinked accusingly behind her.

The oldest technician, a woman called Morag with white hair packed under a wool hat, shrugged.

“You lot grew up wanting to fly for the CIA. We grew up waiting for the Soviets. Everyone deserves one last ridiculous operation.”

Switch stared at her.

Then she laughed so hard she had to sit on a crate labelled FRAGILE BERRIES.

The scandal lasted nine days.

The Civil Aviation Authority issued a statement of magnificent dryness. The council commissioned a security review. The newspapers enjoyed themselves indecently. One columnist declared that nuclear-powered strawberry drones had exposed the dangers of “rural techno-utopianism”, then admitted in paragraph eleven that the berries were excellent.

Morag and her co-conspirators received community service, which they served by teaching cyber-resilience to the drone pilots. Within a month, the container farm had a new training module called Operation Dessert Storm, which management tried to rename and failed.

Years later, when people told the story of how Lewis became the strawberry capital of the North Atlantic, they usually began with the reactor. That made sense. The SMR had changed the arithmetic. Heat, light, power, certainty. It had turned stone and weather into a platform.

Some began with the bunkers. That made sense too. They had made agriculture look like Cold War infrastructure and somehow produced fruit tender enough to silence a room.

Others began with the gannets, the winged couriers that stitched the island to the mainland in clean white arcs, carrying sweetness across the Minch while ferries groaned and gulls complained.

Switch preferred to begin with the kids.

“The future came,” she would say, older now, hair silver at the temples, still wearing absurd headphones, “because we had a generation who thought they were training for secret war and accidentally trained for logistics.”

She would say this to apprentices who had never known the island without the reactor plume, without the bunker lights glowing under turf, without UAVs crossing the morning sky. They would grin politely, the way young people do when history starts talking with its hands.

Then she would give them control of a gannet.

Outside, Lewis would keep doing what Lewis had always done: wind over moor, rain on stone, salt in the air, old songs hiding in new machines. Inside Strawberry Bunker Seventeen, bees would wake under artificial dawn. Beyond Stornoway, a white UAV would rise from the launch strip, cargo pods full, wings catching the pale light.

And somewhere on the mainland, before lunch, someone would bite into a Lewis strawberry and taste the impossible: summer, engineered under a sky that had never agreed to it.
